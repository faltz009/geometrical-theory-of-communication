What is this?

In 1948 Claude Shannon’s A Mathematical Theory of Communication showed how to send any message, by agreeing on its symbols beforehand, and Warren Weaver named the two problems it left open: whether the meaning arrives, and whether it has the effect intended. A Geometrical Theory of Communication takes up those two problems by putting back what Shannon set aside, the reference a meaning is checked against. 01 / What is information?

Something you didn’t know before

Asking what information is already assumes the question has an object: that the word points to something, whether we call it data or knowledge, something that exists in some sense, even if abstract.

So let us say information exists, and assume I’ve seen it and measured it, and I absolutely know what it is. How do I transfer that knowledge to you, the reader? How do I give you information about information? And how do you know that knowledge is true?

01 / What is information?

Everything is written in information

Every science records what it observes and passes on what it knows through descriptions, so every field has its own answer to the question.

Heinrich Hertz opened The Principles of Mechanics by saying, “We form for ourselves images or symbols of external objects.” We reason with those descriptions, then compare their consequences with what happens in the world.

But the description is also information. How do we check that it carries what it describes? Every science faces this question, including a science of information, and we will return to it.

Hertz, The Principles of Mechanics, introduction, 1894 (English translation, 1899).

01 / What is information?

Say I point to a lighter on the desk between us: we both see it, and we both know it is there. What does that mean?

The word lighter points to an object in the world, and our two perspectives on that object still point the same word at the same thing. I could call it an igniter and you would receive the same information through a different symbol: the word carries a meaning we can both already reach.

Information about what? Always a relation: a thing, how we observe it, and the sign we use to convey it. Otherwise, we get paradoxes and tautologies.

01 / What is information?

When you look at the lighter there are three parts: you, the lighter and your looking at it, the observer, the observed and the observation.

Nothing exists in isolation: a number is a point, the origin it is counted from and the line on which it is drawn; a message needs a sender, a receiver and the signal between them; and a particle’s mass is how it resists a push, its charge how it pulls and repels other charges.

A Mathematical Theory of Communication defines information in terms of difference or change: a string of only 0s is the same as a single 0, and a string of only 1s the same as a single 1.

Once both appear, the string holds the 0s, the 1s and the relations between them, and that is where information lives, in the difference.

01 / What is information?

Why do the fields look separate?

The lighter, our view of it and the word for it are three places on one relation, and each place asks its own question:

  • the thing: what exists? physics and metaphysics
  • the observation: how do we observe it, and know it? measurement and epistemology
  • the sign: how do we represent it? linguistics

Each field answers one of these questions from its own place on the relation, so from inside any one field the others look like separate subjects, and what they share is the relation itself.

How do I tell you what I know?

A Communication Problem

02 / A communication problem

Meta-language

As soon as I know something and set out to tell you, we are inside a communication problem. What I know about the lighter reaches you only as a sign, and you have to be able to check it.

But a single sign can point to different things: a bank can be where you keep your money or where you sit beside a river. You can hear the word perfectly and still need to know which one I mean.

The same holds for every measurement, proof and book: everything we know has to be communicated.

Here, we use language to talk about language itself. How do I convey to you information about information?

02 / A communication problem

Warren Weaver set out the communication problem in three levels, in The Mathematical Theory of Communication (1949).

02 / A communication problem

The symbols, and what they carry

Level A is about getting the symbols across: every letter I send arrives as the same letter on your side. Level B is about getting across what the symbols carry, the thing I meant arriving as the thing you understand.

The two come apart easily. We can teach a parrot to repeat the whole of Nietzsche’s work, every word in the right order, and it won’t be having any existential crises: the symbols arrived perfectly and the meaning stayed behind.

Level C is the step after that, whether what you understood changes what you do the way I intended.

02 / A communication problem

Shannon solved Level A, and this work deals with Level B, so from here on information is meaning: the thing I meant, arriving as the thing you understand, unchanged from my side to yours. It is the identity A = A, held between two people.

02 / A communication problem

If I tell you a star is a star, nothing changes for you. The identity holds, and no change travels with it.

If I tell you Venus is the morning star, and you did not know it before, something changes over the identity of Venus: you now reach the same planet by a second route. That change is what the message carried.

Frege opened his 1892 paper on sense and reference with exactly this contrast, between a = a and a = b.

02 / A communication problem

The univocity of language

If I say A, you must understand A for that meaning to have been communicated.

Language allows a little variation, about love or gravity, and still nearly every word lands the same way for both of us. The more precise the subject, the tighter the agreement, until in mathematics it becomes exact.

There, decoding is a function: each word picks out exactly one meaning. Several words may pick out the same one, as igniter and lighter do, so the exact requirement is the round trip, decode(encode(A)) = A.

How did we learn to send any message at all?

Shannon’s Bit

03 / Shannon’s bit

How did Shannon and Weaver solve Level A?

Level A is where to start, since what Shannon set aside to solve it is exactly what Level B needs back. He set meaning aside, on purpose. Shannon wrote in 1948 that the semantic aspects of communication “are irrelevant to the engineering problem”; the task was to recover, at one end, the message selected at the other.

Information, in Weaver’s words, is “a measure of one’s freedom of choice when one selects a message.” It belongs to the whole set of messages that could have been sent: it “relates not so much to what you do say, as to what you could say.”

03 / Shannon’s bit

What is in Shannon’s theory?

Shannon’s 1948 theory has three parts:

  • the bit, the unit: a choice between two, 0 or 1, whose meaning both sides agree on beforehand
  • entropy, the measure: how much we do not know about what will be sent, counted from the odds of every possibility
  • noise, the channel: whatever changes a message on the way, and the capacity, the most a channel can carry despite it
03 / Shannon’s bit

Weaver’s example: two possible messages, the whole King James Bible or the single word “Yes”.

One is enormous and the other is one word, and to the theory they weigh the same, because only the choice between them is measured.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Both sides agree beforehand that 0 selects the Bible and 1 selects “Yes”, or a closed relay one and an open relay the other. A single signal then tells the receiver which was chosen.

That choice between two is one bit, John Tukey’s contraction of binary digit.

03 / Shannon’s bit

How bits become complete

With just two symbols and the idea of position, binary can write every whole number, since each place is worth twice the place to its right:

1101 = 1·8 + 1·4 + 0·2 + 1·1 = 13

The power is in the positions, and every bit added doubles what a string can reach.

Once there are numbers, they can stand for anything both sides agree on. Give each letter a number, and a word becomes a string of bits:

A ↦ 65 ↦ 01000001

A photograph works the same way, with numbers for the colour of each dot, and so does sound, so two symbols and position are enough to write any message. The same step, numbers standing for symbols, is the one Gödel used in 1931 to let a system of symbols talk about itself.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Shannon’s entropy

Entropy measures how much we do not know.

It is counted from the odds of every possibility.

A coin that always lands heads leaves nothing unknown, since you knew the answer before it fell, and a fair coin leaves one bit unknown, since either side is as likely as the other.

The same count describes a gas in a box: one molecule could be anywhere, so the odds of finding it in a given corner are small, and the odds of finding all of them in one corner are so small that it never happens, since the spread-out arrangements outnumber it by far.

Entropy counts how many ways things could be and how likely each one is, so it never depends on what the possibilities are, only on how many there are and their odds.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Entropy across the sciences

Shannon took the name from physics, where Boltzmann had already written the same count for gas molecules, and since then entropy has become a measure every science uses on its own subject.

  • Thermodynamicsheat flows from hot to cold, and time runs one way
  • Biologyliving things keep their order by giving disorder away
  • Cosmologya black hole holds as much of it as any region of space can
  • Ecologythe diversity of a forest is measured with it
  • Machine learninglanguage models learn by lowering it
03 / Shannon’s bit

Every bit is somewhere

A bit is always held by something physical: a high or low voltage on a wire, a magnet pointing one way or the other on a disk, a pit or a flat spot on a disc, a hole punched or left in a card.

So every bit sits somewhere in space and takes time to travel, which means it has a shape, and looking at that shape shows what the bit keeps and what it leaves out.

What a bit holds is one difference, this or that, 0 or 1, and information in Shannon’s sense is counted in such differences. So what does a difference look like in space?

03 / Shannon’s bit

Seeing the bit geometrically

A bit is two marks on a line, 0 as the reference, held fixed, and 1 as the alternative, one unit away, which already makes it a measurement against a chosen unit.

Bits compose only if each one is the same size and varies without moving the others, which makes them perpendicular, since two directions that share a unit and leave each other’s reading untouched meet at a right angle.

Why do we always cut a pizza into four or eight slices? Do we take out a compass to measure the right angles? Perpendicularity exists in space before we assign a symbol to it.

Even a single bit carries that right angle, because every point a unit away from 0 could be called 1, those points trace a circle around the reference, and the perpendicular at 1 is the circle’s tangent, the one direction in which the reading holds.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Computers already compute like this

With one right angle per bit, two bits give the four corners of a square, three give the eight corners of a cube, and each further bit adds one more perpendicular direction. Every string of bits a computer holds is one corner of such a cube, measured from the same starting corner, 00…0.

Computer science works on this cube directly:

  • logic gates are functions on its corners
  • the distance between two messages is the number of edges between their corners
  • error-correcting codes keep valid messages far apart on it
  • some machines were wired in its shape
03 / Shannon’s bit

What does noise do to a message?

Noise is anything that changes a message on its way from sender to receiver, like a scratch on a disc or static on the radio turning a 0 into a 1.

Shannon treated noise in the same 1948 paper, where he showed that every channel has a capacity, the most bits per second it can carry with as few errors as we want, and that this capacity depends on how strong the noise is compared with the signal.

03 / Shannon’s bit

A signal has a structure

110 and 011 hold the same symbols in a different order, and read from 000, one bit per axis, they end on different corners of the cube.

Read as a whole, a string is a place in a structure: 10 and 01 are different corners of the square, and 1101 and 1110 different corners of the four-dimensional cube, so the order carries the meaning.

Read linearly, one symbol at a time, the same string is a sequence of choices, each with its odds, which is what entropy counts.

03 / Shannon’s bit

What the bit gave us

Level A was solved: as long as both sides agree beforehand on what each symbol stands for, any message can cross any distance and arrive symbol for symbol, and that one idea built digital communication.

Level A · the technical problemsolved, 1948
Level B · the semantic problemopen
Level C · the effectiveness problemopen

How do we make sure the meaning is carried with the sign? That is Level B, and it is now the open problem.

03 / Shannon’s bit

So, what is information?

Shannon’s bit measures change, since entropy counts the differences between what could have been sent, while the reference those differences are counted from is agreed beforehand. Level B asks for the other half, what stays the same through the change, the way the lighter stays the lighter whether I call it a lighter or an igniter.

What is a thing? What is a number? What is an identity? What is the meaning of something? And what do you know about it? These questions are signs that point to the same object:

Information is invariance over change.

A = A

Change over invariance is the same relation read from its other end: what changes is measured against what stays, and what stays is recognized through what changes.

The rest of this work rebuilds Shannon’s three parts with the invariance kept: the geometric bit, closure, and an exact account of noise.

What is the shape of information?

The Geometric Bit

04 / The geometric bit

What symbol preserves invariance over change?

To tell that a thing is the same thing we compare it, checking what stayed the same and what changed, and Shannon’s bit already implies a figure that holds every relationship the comparison needs.

The circle is the shape of information.

04 / The geometric bit

Information exists in spacetime

Everything that exists, exists in spacetime, and that includes us: the person who thinks, the thought, the word that carries it and the page it is written on are all things happening somewhere, at some time.

So a thought, a piece of information or a meaning is made of what spacetime offers, and whatever carries it from one person to another has to follow spacetime’s shape.

The symbol that preserves invariance over change is geometric because spacetime has geometry.

04 / The geometric bit

The alphabet of space

The alphabet of binary is 0 and 1, and the alphabet of DNA is four bases (A, T, C, G) and their folding organization. The alphabet of space is perpendicularity and symmetry, and the circle’s four points 1, i, −1 and −i carry both, where i is a quarter turn: multiplying a direction by i turns it by a right angle.

  • perpendicularity is marked by the letters, since 1 and i are a right angle apart and moving along 1 changes nothing along i, which is difference
  • symmetry is marked by the signs, since 1 and −1 are the same unit on opposite sides of the centre and knowing where 1 is tells you exactly where −1 is, which is sameness

Both perpendicularity and symmetry exist as physical phenomena in space before we assign symbols to them.

Perpendicularity and symmetry are what physics writes as thermodynamics and conservation laws, since entropy counts the independent ways a system can vary, each perpendicular to the others, and Emmy Noether showed in 1918 that every continuous symmetry carries a conserved quantity.

04 / The geometric bit

π is symmetry, e is perpendicularity

Take a point and let it change, and the change can point two ways. If it points along where the point already is, the point grows by building on what it already has, which is e, and if it always points at a right angle to where the point is, the point never gets closer or farther and goes around instead, which is eiθ, so e is perpendicularity accumulated.

Going around, the point reaches the mirror of where it started after exactly π, eiπ = −1, and itself again after 2π, so π is symmetry, the distance from a thing to its opposite. Euler’s identity is the alphabet of space written as one sentence: perpendicular change carried through one symmetry lands on the opposite, and the unit with its opposite returns to the reference, 0.

π is symmetry and e is perpendicularity.

04 / The geometric bit

The transcendental bridge

Shannon’s bit goes from the discrete toward the continuous by adding bits: each new bit doubles the number of levels, so 4 bits give 16 levels and 10 bits give 1,024, and a smooth quantity is approached by using more and more of them.

The geometric bit goes the other way and starts from the continuous. The rotation is measured by π and e, which are transcendental, since they lie outside every equation with whole-number coefficients, and yet together they give e2πi = 1 exactly, a whole number.

So the whole numbers come out of the rotation as the count of its completed returns.

04 / The geometric bit

How do we know a thing is a thing?

A = A seems too obvious to need a check, until we ask how we would carry one out. First we tell A apart from everything around it, this and not that, and then we recognize it again somewhere else or in another form, this here equals this there, so the identity principle is a process that needs both steps.

Perpendicularity tells things apart and symmetry recognizes them again, which is the invariance over change we met with the lighter.

Shannon’s bit implies the perpendicular and throws away the angle, keeping only which side of the line a reading falls on.

The circle keeps the angle, so it lets us quantize both relationships precisely, how far a reading has rotated from its reference and when it has come back to it, and transmit them.

04 / The geometric bit

The turn preserves identity

When we both look at the lighter from different places, what reaches each of us is the light around it, a field with a value at every point, and each of us reads that field at our own points, mine on one grid and yours on a grid turned by the angle between us.

Shannon’s sampling theorem says that a field with limited detail is fixed exactly by its values at enough points, so you and I rebuild the same lighter from different samples, and the turn between us changes which points we read and not what we rebuild.

At Level B, the meaning I send has to arrive as the meaning you understand, and here the meaning is the lighter itself, which you rebuild exactly from your own samples, so the identity arrives as it was sent.

04 / The geometric bit

Composing geometric bits: hyperspheres

Shannon’s bits compose by adding one perpendicular direction per bit, which builds the corners of a square, a cube and a hypercube.

Geometric bits compose by holding their rotations together to one unit of length, so one rotation traces the circle, two trace the three-sphere, a sphere one dimension beyond the ones we can picture, and n rotations trace a sphere of 2n − 1 dimensions.

Every point on these spheres is a list of rotations, and a circle runs through every point, the rotation they all share, so the geometric bit stays whole inside every sphere it composes. What these compositions can do, and where they stop, is the subject of section 06.

And what has the shape of a hypersphere?

Everything

04 / The geometric bit

The shape of matter

The three-sphere is the shape general relativity gives a closed universe, and it was the first shape Einstein gave the whole cosmos, in 1917, a space with no edge where a traveller who keeps going straight comes back to where they started.

A point of the three-sphere is two rotations held together, and if we keep only how the two compare and ignore the turn they share, each point lands on a point of an ordinary sphere, with all the points that land on the same place forming a circle. So the three-sphere is filled with circles, one for each point of the ordinary sphere and each linked with every other, which is the Hopf fibration.

Physics keeps finding it: Dirac’s magnetic monopole is built on it, every qubit is read on its sphere, and light can be tied into its linked circles.

04 / The geometric bit

Everything that persists is a rotation

Something persists when it keeps coming back to the same state, and the simplest motion that always comes back is a point going around a circle, which seen from the side rises and falls as a wave, so whatever lasts, at the scale we can hold and below it, is a rotation or several rotations combined:

  • springs, pendulums and circuits oscillate as the shadow of a rotation
  • sound and water waves are rotations spread over space and carried over time
  • light is an electric and a magnetic field rotating together at right angles
  • every quantum possibility carries a rotating arrow, and opposite arrows cancel
  • atoms keep only the orbits where the rotation closes
04 / The geometric bit

Computing through motion

Physics describes motion in three theories, and composed rotation sits at the centre of each:

  • Classicalevery conserved motion that repeats runs on circles, each coordinate an angle advancing at its own rate
  • QuantumSchrödinger’s equation rotates a state without changing its length
  • Relativitychanging speed is a rotation in spacetime

A quantum state is n rotations held to one unit of length whose shared rotation changes nothing we measure, so a qubit is two geometric bits and every wave function, from an atom to a molecule, lives on a hypersphere of rotations.

04 / The geometric bit

Mathematics is already written in rotations

Much of mathematics already works with composed rotation, and each of these branches is a tool that other fields depend on:

  • complex analysis, the tool behind the prime number theorem, runs on rotation by a right angle
  • Fourier analysis writes every signal as a sum of rotations
  • trigonometry, the sines and cosines in every calculation, is the circle read along two perpendicular axes
  • the symmetries physics is written in are groups of rotations, the circle first among them
04 / The geometric bit

We already use it everywhere

We compute with composed rotation every day, in the technology around us:

  • Phonesphones and Wi-Fi send bits as points on the circle
  • Powerthe grid runs on rotating voltage and is computed as rotations
  • Medicinean MRI scan reads protons rotating in a magnetic field
  • Mediapictures and sound are stored as sums of waves
  • Radioantennas are matched by rotating around a disc, the Smith chart
  • Motionrobots, phones and spacecraft track which way they face with quaternions
04 / The geometric bit

Closure, the other half of entropy

Closure measures what we know: the return of a distinction to its reference.

Entropy counts what could have been sent, and closure checks that what arrived is what was sent.

Entropy measures what we do not know, and closure measures what we do know, the part of a message that comes back to its reference. Shannon’s bit leaves that reference to an agreement made beforehand, and closure can check it, because the reference now travels with the message.

04 / The geometric bit

Entropy and closure are one measure

Temperature needs both hot and cold, since each is measured against the other, and information works the same way: a change is measured against something that stays the same. Entropy measures the change, how much could vary, and closure measures what stays, what comes back to its reference, so each one needs the other to mean anything.

That puts closure everywhere entropy is. A gas spreads through a room while its energy stays the same, an engine’s heat flows while the engine comes back to the same state every cycle, a cell gives away disorder to keep its own shape, and a message is counted against an alphabet both sides share, so each time entropy counts what changed, something held still to count it against.

The two sit together in Euler’s identity: e carries the change and π the return, and one full rotation holds both.

04 / The geometric bit

Closure across the sciences

Entropy runs through every science, and so does its other half, found again and again under a different name, as whatever comes back to where it started:

  • Lifea living thing feeds on negative entropy, the order it keeps restoring
  • Heatenergy and entropy return to the same value around every closed cycle
  • Circuitsthe voltages around any closed loop add up to zero
  • Chemistrya reaction gives the same heat by any path between the same two states
  • Controla feedback loop keeps bringing a system back to its set point

Each field measures the same thing, how a quantity comes back to its reference after a change, and it has never had a common name or a precise construction.

04 / The geometric bit

Shannon’s bit and entropy are a reading of it

Recording which side of a diameter holds the mark gives one bit, and two perpendicular diameters give four quadrants, the four corners of the square, whose labels we can keep while setting the exact angle aside.

Once those alternatives and their probabilities are given, Shannon’s entropy applies to the choice among them, and the square, the cube and every computation on their corners follow as before, so the bit is the circle read at its quadrants, with the rotation and the reference set aside.

One science

05 / Operationalizing identity

Easier to say where it isn’t

Look back at the last screens, physics at every scale, all three theories of motion, much of mathematics, the technology we use every day and closure in every science, and each of them runs on the same sentence, eiπ + 1 = 0, most of the time without naming it.

It is easier to say where it is not yet written: wherever we describe things without mathematical notation, in natural language and across the humanities.

And even there the line is blurring, since language models already write the position of every word as a rotation.

05 / Operationalizing identity

A physical law

So what is it? A relation that all of these descriptions rely on, and that holds in every one of them, is what physics calls a law. Newton’s laws and the laws of thermodynamics count as physical because we can identify the quantities, measure their relationship and check that the description keeps what the measurements establish, and Euler’s identity passes the same test, since each of its symbols is a measurement anyone can repeat.

The symbols are agreed on, since the 1 could be written with any mark and the right angle is 90 only because we chose degrees, but what they point to is not: the unit comes back whole and discrete after every full rotation, and two equal steps reach its opposite in any notation. We put notation on the objects and relationships that are useful and coherent, so the law is the relationship, the shape of information, and the symbol is the name we agreed to give it.

Euler’s identity is a physical law: it records the measured relationship of continuous rotation and closure.

05 / Operationalizing identity

Space curves the same way everywhere

Wrap a string around anything round, a cup, a wheel or a planet, lay it against the diameter, and it measures three diameters and a little more, 3.14159…, whatever the size, whatever unit you measure in and whoever does the measuring.

An atom, a planet and a galaxy all curve the same way, and anyone in the universe who can hold a string finds the same π, so π belongs to space itself and not to our notation, the same symmetry at every size.

eiπ + 1 = 0
05 / Operationalizing identity

e is measured in every lab

Anything that changes in proportion to what it already has follows e: a radioactive sample loses the same fraction every second, a hot cup cools toward the room, a charged capacitor drains and a colony of bacteria grows, and every lab that times one of these finds the same e, 2.71828…, in any unit and whoever does the timing.

Rotation is the same accumulation with the change pointing sideways, since in eiθ each step is at a right angle to where the point already is, so it neither grows nor fades and goes around instead, and the same e that times the decay times the rotation.

eiπ + 1 = 0
05 / Operationalizing identity

The right angle is in space

Hang a weight on a string and let water settle, and the string meets the surface at a right angle anywhere on Earth, so gravity gives the right angle at every point before anyone writes it down, and builders check it with a cord knotted into lengths of 3, 4 and 5.

It could not be any other angle, since rotating by a right angle is the one step that, taken twice, reaches the opposite, i² = −1, and the signs of arithmetic, the waves of physics and every rotation composed before rely on that single value.

eiπ + 1 = 0
05 / Operationalizing identity

Every measurement agrees

π is measured with a string, e with a clock and the right angle with a plumb line, and together they mark a few places in space, the full rotation, the half and the quarter. We decide to write 1 at the full rotation and 90° or i at the quarter, so the symbols are ours to choose, while the places they mark belong to space.

There are only a handful of places where a number, once placed, tells the truth about reality, and they are why multiplication, which began with counting sheep, can calculate a radio wave. Every field that composes rotation checks those same places each time it works, when an orbit is predicted, a qubit is read or a phone call arrives.

Separate measurements, made anywhere and at any scale, always meet in the same relation.

05 / Operationalizing identity

What does it mean for identity to be a physical law?

Until now, calling an apple an apple is something we agree on beforehand, the way Shannon’s bit agrees on what 0 and 1 stand for. Tying identity to physics gives meaning a shape.

Plato thought every idea had a form, a perfect shape that every apple and every word for apple points back to, and he placed those forms outside the world. Here the shape is in the world, made of the same rotations as everything else, so it can be measured.

Then “is this still the same thing?” has a measured answer, like “how long is this?”: you line up what arrived against a reference, and the answer is how far it misses.

05 / Operationalizing identity

Language is hard

Words shift between cultures, the same word carries different feelings for different people, and no two people learned love the same way.

Every perspective is valid, so how can we account for all of them? We only need what all of them share, the part every observer agrees on, which in physics is the light cone: what can reach whom, and in what order. That shared reality is spacetime, the same space, the same light, the same apple falling from the same tree, and every meaning is built out of it, so lining up the world lines up the meanings built on it.

Life works the same way. A bacterium, a tree and you are all written with the same four bases of DNA, and nobody agrees on each creature separately, only on four letters and how they combine. We all share one reality because everything that exists is tied to everything else.

05 / Operationalizing identity

Does it still look weird?

Language models already do a rough version of this. Each word becomes a point, and words with similar meanings end up close together, so apple sits near pear and love sits near care. Two languages learned separately line up by a single rotation, well enough to translate.

Models trained on different data, and even on pictures instead of words, end up agreeing on which things relate to which. The shape of meaning comes from the world they all learn from, which is how your words reach me.

05 / Operationalizing identity

The rest is residue

The models find that shape by guessing it from enormous amounts of text, with no fixed reference to check against, and the guessing is expensive:

  • a large model reads over ten trillion words, while a child learns a language from fewer than a hundred million, over 100,000 times less
  • a brain runs on about 20 watts, a dim light bulb, while training a single model the size of GPT-3 used about 1,300 megawatt-hours, what a brain uses in over 7,000 years

Most of that cost is residue: the work of guessing each relation from statistics instead of checking it. The brain computes with rotation and return, and what the models lack is the reference, something fixed to line a meaning up against and check that it came back.

One paradigm for science

Every science passes descriptions from one observer to another, so every science depends on Level B: when a physicist, a biologist or a programmer says “this is the same thing”, they need a way to check it.

A check needs a reference outside what it checks. Euler’s identity, as a physical law, is one: it belongs to no field and no notation, and anyone can measure it with a string, a clock and a plumb line.

Taken as the reference every science checks identity against, it puts all of them under one paradigm. Kuhn called this a paradigm shift: a change in the ground a whole science stands on.

05 / Operationalizing identity

What about incompleteness?

In the 1920s Hilbert hoped mathematics could prove, from its own rules, that it would never contradict itself. In 1931 Gödel showed that it cannot: any system of rules strong enough for arithmetic has true statements it cannot prove, and it cannot prove its own consistency.

His proof turns sentences into numbers, the same step as writing letters as numbers, so the system can talk about itself. But the one who reads that number as a sentence, and sees that it is true, is Gödel, standing outside the system. No system can check itself: the check comes from outside.

For science, the outside is reality. Experiments check theories and measurement checks science, and Euler’s law is a measurement anyone can make, so the checker is the shared world itself. That is also how you can check me: measurement tests where this argument begins, and every step from there is written so that you can follow it.

05 / Operationalizing identity

To exist is to interact

Why can everyone share one reference? Because we all share one reality, and what exists is what can enter a comparison. The lighter on the desk reflects light, resists a hand, exchanges heat and can produce a flame, and those interactions are how we encounter it. What would it mean to say that something existed physically if it could enter no interaction at all?

Something exists physically when it is available to interaction.

The lighter stays on the desk when we look away, and its relationships with the desk, the air and the light carry on.

The observer belongs to the same picture, since the person, the instrument, the inscription and the act of thinking all happen in spacetime. Imagination works the same way: a blender full of carrots gives carrots, so we can imagine a blue lighter out of blue things and lighters we have met, and never a colour no interaction ever gave us.

05 / Operationalizing identity

Nothing is independent

Two things are independent when knowing one tells you nothing at all about the other. Now picture something independent of everything else: no light leaves it, no heat, no push, no trace in any record or any mind. There is no way, even in principle, to tell it apart from nothing.

So whatever exists for anyone is tied to them, however weakly, and physics already says so in one place, since gravity pulls on everything that has energy, which ties every physical thing to every other, and gravity too is written with the rotations we have been following, in a more complex form.

What looks independent is only tied weakly enough to ignore, and perpendicular directions, where moving along one changes nothing along the other, are how that looks up close. The steady averages that statistics relies on never needed independence either, as Markov showed with the letters of a poem.

What did Shannon do with his bit?

Most of Shannon’s 1948 paper is about operating signals, how they combine into messages, how much a channel can carry and what noise does to what is sent, and a signal can carry anything, since every sound, every picture, light itself and the states of atoms are waves.

Shannon’s bit reads a wave, which is a rotation seen from the side, as a list of numbers, the heights of its samples, throws away the closure condition and calculates the entropy of what is left, while the geometric bit keeps the closure and composes with it.

06 / Operating geometric bits

What do geometric signals build?

A geometric signal can describe everything a signal can, and it keeps the closure: it is rotations side by side, each returning to where it started, and held together to one total length the whole signal is a single point on a sphere, two rotations making the three-sphere and n rotations a sphere of 2n − 1 dimensions, the same spheres Shannon drew his channels on in 1949.

Composing signals composes these spheres, so any system written as signals is written on these spheres, each filled with copies of the smaller ones the way the three-sphere is filled with circles, with every part still carrying its reference.

06 / Operating geometric bits

What is a thing?

A number is a thing, and mathematics builds the numbers by rotating through the exponential map eiθ: a full turn returns to 1, and dividing the turn into n equal steps writes the whole numbers in base n, each at its remainder on the circle. In physics a particle is described by the same expression, since its wave is ei(kx − ωt), and in computation the thing is the bit, which in a geometric bit is that same rotation: an angle that says how far it has turned from 1, and whether it has come back to 1.

So a number, a particle and a bit are the same object written by one expression, and each of them stays one thing because its rotation keeps returning to where it started, which is what Euler’s identity e2πi = 1 says. Drawn with time as a third axis, the rotation is Euler’s helix, which seen end-on is the circle and seen from the side is the wave, and since each full turn counts one, the quantum numbers of physics count turns the way the whole numbers do.

06 / Operating geometric bits

Wave or particle?

The rotation that makes a thing has two sides:

  • seen as it moves, it is a wave: continuous, every part of the turn the same as every other, which is symmetry
  • seen as it is counted, it is a particle: one whole return at a time, each a single distinction, which is perpendicularity

Wave and particle, the continuous and the discrete, sameness and difference, closure and entropy are one pair under different names, and the pair is something we observe in space, whatever model describes it: information theory calls it closure and entropy, physics wave–particle duality, mathematics the continuous and the discrete, music a vibration and its notes, and in space itself it is symmetry and perpendicularity.

Physics is already harmony

In 1926 Schrödinger called his paper on quantum mechanics “Quantization as an eigenvalue problem”, which means finding the waves that fit in a space: the shape of a space decides which waves fit in it, and the waves that fit are the only states a particle held in that space can have. Physics already measures them:

  • on a circle, a whole number of waves around: the notes of a string
  • on a sphere, the shapes of an electron around a nucleus, in groups of 1, 3, 5 and 7: the orbitals of chemistry
  • on the three-sphere, the energy levels of hydrogen (Fock, 1935), holding 1, 4, 9 and 16 of those shapes: with spin, the shells of the periodic table

Physics, mathematics and computation are one object, and that object is information.

No isolated things

A thing, at its smallest, is circles composed: a single geometric bit is a circle, but to exist is to interact and nothing exists alone, so every thing we meet, from a particle to a planet, is circles composed into each other, each one linked through the others.

The one object is something larger, the space where all of these compositions can be, the spheres of the next cards, each filled with copies of the one below, and every thing is a part of it, told apart from the others by where it sits.

Why circles and spheres?

Because composing things has to keep what makes each of them a thing, perpendicularity and symmetry, and circles and spheres are the shapes that keep both: on them every motion stays at a right angle to the centre, and every point is the same as every other.

Space, the place where things can be, is itself a thing, so it has the same shape, and space and time are measured to be curved.

06 / Operating geometric bits

The nested fibrations

Composing rotations into each other is multiplying them, the way a quarter turn followed by another quarter turn is i × i = −1, a half turn.

Multiplying keeps perpendicularity and symmetry in exactly four number systems, the real numbers ℝ, the complex numbers ℂ, the quaternions ℍ and the octonions 𝕆, called the division algebras.

The limit theorem, proved by Hurwitz in 1898.

Each division algebra fills a sphere with copies of the sphere below, a Hopf fibration, and Adams proved in 1960 that only these four nest this way, and the nested fibrations they make, the layers in the drawing, are the one object that physics, mathematics and computation project.

Everything we perceive is ℍ, three directions of space and one of time.

06 / Operating geometric bits

A fractal structure

Why do we cut a pizza into four or eight slices? Because those cuts keep both properties at once, the same two we find in every composition: each cut is at a right angle to another, and every slice is the same as the others.

Four slices take one cross, and eight take two crosses, the second a copy of the first turned half a right angle, so the structure repeats inside itself, the way each number system is two copies of the one before: the complex numbers are two real numbers, the quaternions two complex numbers and the octonions two quaternions.

With two crosses, which one is the reference? Measuring from one or from the other gives different answers, so the order in which we group the steps starts to matter: multiplying into the second copy of the quaternions reverses the order of the first, a(bℓ) = (ba)ℓ, which is what losing associativity means. At sixteen slices the four crosses can no longer all keep their right angles, two things can multiply to nothing, and the doubling stops after four levels.

Where are the particles and forces?

The particles and forces of the Standard Model, the theory behind every particle physics experiment so far, sit inside the four division algebras. The symmetry of each force is one of the layers: electromagnetism turns by the unit circle of ℂ, the weak force by the unit three-sphere of ℍ, and the strong force by the symmetries of 𝕆 that keep one direction fixed.

Cohl Furey, building on Geoffrey Dixon, has shown that one generation of quarks and leptons, with their electric and colour charges, comes out of the four multiplied together, ℝ ⊗ ℂ ⊗ ℍ ⊗ 𝕆, and has carried the construction toward all three generations. So the layers built from perpendicularity and symmetry carry the physics already measured, force by force.

The string in string theory

With the one object at its center, string theory works: the string is the geometric bit, the smallest piece of the object, and its dimensions are the layers, since superstrings exist only in 3, 4, 6 and 10 dimensions, two more than the 1, 2, 4 and 8 of ℝ, ℂ, ℍ and 𝕆.

The six extra directions that string theory curls up too small to see then have a place: the string vibrates in the eight directions of the octonions, the layer of the object above ours.

The Ruliad

Stephen Wolfram reached one object from computation, the Ruliad, every possible rule followed in every possible way, and argues that any observer who persists through time, as we do, samples both our physics and our mathematics from it.

Persisting is keeping identity, and keeping identity while composing leaves only the division algebras and their nested fibrations, so they are what a persisting observer finds in the Ruliad, and Jonathan Gorard has shown that its branching histories already have the geometry of quantum states, which for one qubit is the sphere at the base of the complex Hopf fibration.

So the Standard Model, string theory and the Ruliad reach the same object from three sides: from the particles we measure, from the unification of the forces and from computation.

The unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics

In 1960 Eugene Wigner called the success of mathematics in physics unreasonable, since mathematicians develop it for its own beauty and the laws of physics keep turning out to be written in it. The fifth shows why, since mechanics, geometry, arithmetic and music were each reading the same two rotations, an object in space that physics measures as well.

Mathematics has two parts, the object, which is that structure in space, and the symbols we write to represent it, such as 2 : 3, eiθ or ℍ. The symbols are a choice, while the object is the same for everyone who measures it, and mathematics looks abstract when its symbols are taken for the object.

So mathematics is simply the precise version of language: a word such as circle points at the object loosely, and an equation such as eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ points at it exactly, which is why a calculation made on paper holds in the world.

Noise is the field

Noise looks random only when we leave out what caused it, and Shannon’s theory leaves it out on purpose, keeping only how strong the noise is compared with the signal.

What changes a message on its way is something acting on it, which physics calls a field, and once the field is kept the change has a definite outcome: carried around a small loop, anything the field acts on comes back turned by exactly what the field does inside the loop, which is how the strong force is computed.

So with the field kept, what we called noise is a description of the field, an interaction with a deterministic outcome.

What keeps a living thing itself?

A living thing stays itself while every molecule in it is replaced, by comparing what it is with what it should be and correcting the difference.

Identity in the humanities

The mind is a product of the memories held in the brain, memories are stored as information, and information is physical, so thoughts have shapes, and the relations a person draws between them can be measured like any other relations.

Relational frame theory, which grew out of Skinner’s account of verbal behaviour into the leading experimental account of how people derive meaning, is built on the identity principle: sameness, A = A, is its basic relation, and from it people derive relations nobody taught them.

Its applied side, acceptance and commitment therapy, has been tested in more than a thousand randomized trials, and the theory is used to teach language to autistic children and in schools.

So the frontier of psychology’s study of language already stands on A = A, and with a measured reference it becomes a check: a person understood when the relations they derive come back to where they started.
