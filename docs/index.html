01 / The question

Something you didn’t know before

Asking what information is already assumes the question has an object: that the word points to something, whether we call it data or knowledge, something that exists in some sense, even if abstract.

So let us say information exists, and assume I’ve seen it and measured it, and I absolutely know what it is. How do I transfer that knowledge to you, the reader? How do I give you information about information? And how do you know that knowledge is true?

01 / The question

Everything is written in information

Every science records what it observes and passes on what it knows through descriptions, so every field has its own answer to the question.

Heinrich Hertz opened The Principles of Mechanics by saying, “We form for ourselves images or symbols of external objects.” We reason with those descriptions, then compare their consequences with what happens in the world.

But the description is also information. How do we check that it carries what it describes? Every science faces this question, including a science of information, and we will return to it.

Hertz, The Principles of Mechanics, introduction, 1894 (English translation, 1899).

02 / One relation

Say I point to a lighter on the desk between us: we both see it, and we both know it is there. What does that mean?

The word lighter points to an object in the world, and our two perspectives on that object still point the same word at the same thing. I could call it an igniter and you would receive the same information through a different symbol: the word carries a meaning we can both already reach.

Information about what? Always a relation: a thing, how we observe it, and the sign we use to convey it. Otherwise, we get paradoxes and tautologies.

02 / One relation

When you look at the lighter there are three parts: you, the lighter and your looking at it, the observer, the observed and the observation.

Nothing exists in isolation: a number is a point, the origin it is counted from and the line on which it is drawn; a message needs a sender, a receiver and the signal between them; and a particle’s mass is how it resists a push, its charge how it pulls and repels other charges.

A Mathematical Theory of Communication defines information in terms of difference or change: a string of only 0s is the same as a single 0, and a string of only 1s the same as a single 1.

Once both appear, the string holds the 0s, the 1s and the relations between them, and that is where information lives, in the difference.

02 / One relation

The object, our view of it and the symbol for it separate three questions:

What exists?How do we observe it, and know it?How do we represent it, and pass it on?

Each field answers one of them from its own place on the relation, which is why, from inside any one field, the others look like separate subjects.

03 / A communication problem

Meta-language

As soon as I know something and set out to tell you, we are inside a communication problem. What I know about the lighter reaches you only as a sign, and you have to be able to check it.

But a single sign can point to different things: a bank can be where you keep your money or where you sit beside a river. You can hear the word perfectly and still need to know which one I mean.

The same holds for every measurement, proof and book: everything we know has to be communicated.

Here, we use language to talk about language itself. How do I convey to you information about information?

03 / A communication problem

Warren Weaver set out the communication problem in three levels, in The Mathematical Theory of Communication (1949).

03 / A communication problem

At Level B, information is meaning: the thing I meant, arriving as the thing you understand, unchanged from my side to yours. It is the identity A = A, held between two people.

03 / A communication problem

If I tell you a star is a star, nothing changes for you. The identity holds, and no change travels with it.

If I tell you Venus is the morning star, and you did not know it before, something changes over the identity of Venus: you now reach the same planet by a second route. That change is what the message carried.

Frege opened his 1892 paper on sense and reference with exactly this contrast, between a = a and a = b.

03 / A communication problem

The univocity of language

If I say A, you must understand A for that meaning to have been communicated.

Language allows a little variation, about love or gravity, and still nearly every word lands the same way for both of us. The more precise the subject, the tighter the agreement, until in mathematics it becomes exact.

There, decoding is a function: each word picks out exactly one meaning. Several words may pick out the same one, as igniter and lighter do, so the exact requirement is the round trip, decode(encode(A)) = A.

04 / Shannon’s bit

How did Shannon and Weaver solve Level A?

By setting meaning aside, on purpose. Shannon wrote in 1948 that the semantic aspects of communication “are irrelevant to the engineering problem”; the task was to recover, at one end, the message selected at the other.

Information, in Weaver’s words, is “a measure of one’s freedom of choice when one selects a message.” It belongs to the whole set of messages that could have been sent: it “relates not so much to what you do say, as to what you could say.”

04 / Shannon’s bit

Weaver’s example: two possible messages, the whole King James Bible or the single word “Yes”.

One is enormous and the other is one word, and to the theory they weigh the same, because only the choice between them is measured.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Both sides agree beforehand that 0 selects the Bible and 1 selects “Yes”, or a closed relay one and an open relay the other. A single signal then tells the receiver which was chosen.

That choice between two is one bit, John Tukey’s contraction of binary digit.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Seeing the bit geometrically

A bit is two marks on a line, 0 as the reference, held fixed, and 1 as the alternative, one unit away, which already makes it a measurement against a chosen unit.

Bits compose only if each one is the same size and varies without moving the others, which makes them perpendicular, since two directions that share a unit and leave each other’s reading untouched meet at a right angle.

Why do we always cut a pizza into four or eight slices? Do we take out a compass to measure the right angles? Perpendicularity exists in space before we assign a symbol to it.

Even a single bit carries that right angle, because every point a unit away from 0 could be called 1, those points trace a circle around the reference, and the perpendicular at 1 is the circle’s tangent, the one direction in which the reading holds.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Computers already compute like this

With one right angle per bit, two bits give the four corners of a square, three give the eight corners of a cube, and each further bit adds one more perpendicular direction. Every string of bits a computer holds is one corner of such a cube, measured from the same starting corner, 00…0.

Computer science works on this cube directly:

  • logic gates are functions on its corners
  • the distance between two messages is the number of edges between their corners
  • error-correcting codes keep valid messages far apart on it
  • some machines were wired in its shape
04 / Shannon’s bit

A signal has a structure

110 and 011 hold the same symbols in a different order, and read from 000, one bit per axis, they end on different corners of the cube.

Read as a whole, a string is a place in a structure: 10 and 01 are different corners of the square, and 1101 and 1110 different corners of the four-dimensional cube, so the order carries the meaning.

Information is that structure.

Read linearly, one symbol at a time, the same string is a line of yes-or-no choices, and counting those choices is what Shannon measured.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Shannon’s entropy

Entropy measures how hard a source is to predict.

In bits, it is the number of yes-or-no questions it takes, on average, to find what was sent.

A coin that always lands heads tells you nothing when it lands, since you knew the answer before it fell, and a fair coin tells you one full bit, since you could not have guessed it.

With eight equally likely messages, three questions always find the one that was sent, and when some are more likely than others, asking about those first takes fewer questions on average. The count never depends on what the messages say, only on how many there could be and how likely each one is.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Entropy across the sciences

Shannon took the name from physics, where Boltzmann had already written the same count for gas molecules, and since then entropy has become a measure every science uses on its own subject.

  • Thermodynamicsheat flows from hot to cold, and time runs one way
  • Biologyliving things keep their order by giving disorder away
  • Cosmologya black hole holds as much of it as any region of space can
  • Ecologythe diversity of a forest is measured with it
  • Machine learninglanguage models learn by lowering it

05 / The geometric bit

So, what is information?

What is a thing? What is a number? What is an identity? What is the meaning of something? And what do you know about it? These questions are signs that point to the same object:

Information is invariance over change.

A = A

Change over invariance is the same relation read from its other end: what changes is measured against what stays, and what stays is recognized through what changes.

Shannon’s bit keeps the change and assumes the invariance, agreed beforehand when the labels are assigned, and the rest of this work gives that invariance a form we can check.

05 / The geometric bit

What symbol preserves invariance over change?

To tell that a thing is the same thing we compare it, checking what stayed the same and what changed, and Shannon’s bit already implies a figure that holds every relationship the comparison needs.

The circle is the shape of information.

05 / The geometric bit

The alphabet of space

The alphabet of binary is 0 and 1, and the alphabet of DNA is four bases (A, T, C, G) and their folding organization. The alphabet of space is perpendicularity and symmetry, and the circle’s four poles carry both, since 1 and −1 are symmetric, the same unit on opposite sides of the centre, and 1 and i are perpendicular, a quarter turn apart.

Perpendicularity separates two changes, which is difference, and symmetry keeps every length and right angle while the whole circle turns, which is sameness.

Both perpendicularity and symmetry exist as physical phenomena in space before we assign symbols to them.

Perpendicularity and symmetry are what physics writes as thermodynamics and conservation laws, since entropy counts the independent ways a system can vary, each perpendicular to the others, and Emmy Noether showed in 1918 that every continuous symmetry carries a conserved quantity.

05 / The geometric bit

How do we check that A = A?

Checking an identity takes two checks, telling the thing apart from everything else, this and not that, and recognizing it again somewhere else or in another form, this here equals this there. Perpendicularity performs the first check and symmetry the second, and together they give the invariance over change we saw with the lighter, where the views, the words and the people changed while the lighter stayed the same.

Shannon’s bit implies the perpendicular and throws away the angle, keeping only which side of the line a reading falls on. The circle keeps the angle, so it lets us quantize both relationships precisely, how far a reading has turned from its reference and when it has come back to it, and transmit them.

05 / The geometric bit

Continuous and discrete

We write the turn with π and e, π for how far it has gone in space and e for how it accumulates in time. Both are transcendental, which means no arithmetic of whole numbers ever reaches them, so they belong wholly to the continuous, and yet together they give e2πi = 1, a whole number exactly, so the circle passes from the continuous to the discrete with no approximation.

Shannon’s bit goes the other way, reaching toward the continuous by composing discreteness, since each added bit doubles the levels and a continuous quantity is approached by ever more of them.

05 / The geometric bit

The turn preserves identity

Say we both look at the lighter from different places, and as we move around it our views keep changing, yet at every moment the angle between us is an exact quantity, so turning your view back by that angle gives mine, and the lighter each of us sees is the same lighter.

At Level B, the meaning I send has to arrive as the meaning you understand, and here that meaning is the lighter itself, which stays the same while our places, our views and our words for it change. Since the angle between our frames can be stated exactly, you can undo it and reach what I meant, so the identity arrives as it was sent.

05 / The geometric bit

What is a number?

Let 1 mark the starting radius and let multiplication by i make a quarter turn. Two turns reach the opposite pole, so i² = −1, the third reaches −i, and the fourth returns to 1, where the count carries on, so i4 and i8 land where i0 began.

The same movement gives two readings, where the point is and how many times it has come back, and every return meets the same criterion, arriving back at the reference, just as every step along the number line has the same length.

A number is a closed distinction, a one.

e2πin = 1 ⟺ n ∈ ℤ

Physical reality presents it as a count of completed returns, in every oscillation, every orbit and every tick of a clock.

05 / The geometric bit

Reading Euler’s identity

Euler’s identity is a sentence about something we can do. Start at 1, the reference, and let the change always point at a right angle to where we are, which is what i asks for, accumulating continuously, which is what e describes, until the turn has gone half way round, π in lengths of the radius: we arrive at −1, and adding where we started to where we arrived leaves nothing, eiπ + 1 = 0.

Every symbol names a step of that operation, so anyone who performs it recovers the identity whatever notation they learned, and its full-turn form, e2πi = 1, is the check that the operation closes.

Starting from any radius A, the full turn returns it, so A = A becomes something we do.

You can draw the two perpendicular diameters while I follow four quarter turns, and we can compare where the poles lie, the angles between them and the return to the start. The drawing and the formula lead to the same checks, so what we understood is established by what we can reproduce, which is the account of identity Level B needs.

06 / Conclusion

Shannon’s bit is a reading of it

Recording which side of a diameter holds the mark gives one bit, and two perpendicular diameters give four quadrants, the four corners of the square, whose labels we can keep while setting the exact angle aside.

Once those alternatives and their probabilities are given, Shannon’s measure applies to the choice among them, and the square, the cube and every computation on their corners follow as before. The bit is the circle read at its quadrants, with the turn and the reference set aside, and the circle keeps both.

06 / Conclusion

Composing geometric bits

Shannon’s bits compose into cubes, one perpendicular direction for each bit, and geometric bits compose into spheres. A pair of circles, two complex numbers held together to one unit of length, makes the three-dimensional sphere of the quaternions, whose turns keep the order in which they happen, and doubling again gives the seven-dimensional sphere of the octonions.

This is the tower of algebras, the real numbers, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions, each carrying the circle inside it, and at every level a Hopf map reads the larger sphere down onto a smaller one, the way Shannon’s bit reads the circle at its quadrants.

06 / Conclusion

Physics already runs like this

A turn watched from the side is an oscillation, since the height of the turning point rises and falls as a wave and the speed of the turn is the wave’s frequency. Wherever physics describes something that repeats, it writes the turn, eiωt, and measures its shadow.

Classical mechanics, quantum mechanics and relativity all work on this circle.

  • springs, pendulums and circuits oscillate as the shadow of a turn
  • every quantum possibility carries a turning arrow, and opposite arrows cancel
  • atoms keep only the orbits where the turn closes
  • changing speed is turning in spacetime
  • every signal is a sum of turns, and phones send bits as points on the circle
06 / Conclusion

Euler’s identity is a law

Why recognize Newton’s law or the laws of thermodynamics as physical while treating Euler’s identity as a mathematical convenience? The standard should be the same for both, identify the quantities, measure their relationship and check that the description preserves what the measurements establish, and the ratio of the circle, the perpendicular turn and the return all pass it.

Euler’s identity is a physical law: it records the measured relationship of continuous turning and closure.

Accounts of the same physical turn must agree about its result, in any notation.

Thermodynamics uses the same exponential for its own composition, since independent systems at one temperature add their energies while their weights, e−E/kT, multiply, just as turns add their angles while their exponentials multiply.

06 / Conclusion

Closure, the counterpart of entropy

Closure is the return of a distinction to its reference, which makes an identity repeatable.

Entropy counts the arrangements a state could have been, and closure keeps the one it is, so information lives between them, since what only spreads keeps nothing to recognize and what only returns tells us nothing new.

Schrödinger called what a living thing takes in to keep its order negative entropy, and in 1953 Brillouin identified information with it, so physics has carried this counterpart all along under the name negentropy. Causality needs it as well, because a cause has to stay itself to be the same cause while its effect differs from it, so closure is the condition for anything to be carried from one state to the next.

06 / Conclusion

Level C: did it work?

Weaver’s third level asks whether the meaning that arrived affects conduct in the way the sender intended, and that is the check of Level B. If I ask you for the lighter and you hand me the lighter, your action has brought the meaning back to me, and I can compare what came back with what I meant.

It is the same return as the full turn: the signal crosses at Level A, the meaning is recovered at Level B, and the action closes the loop at Level C, where the sender recognizes A = A, so with the geometric bit all three levels become checks we can perform.

What can be proved?

What did Gödel prove?

Gödel asked whether a formal system could establish its own truth. Inside the system, his sentence is an ordinary statement about numbers, that no number has a certain arithmetic property, and it says I am unprovable only through the coding Gödel invented, the map from sentences to numbers. That map is not in the system. Gödel holds it, so the self-reference is a relation of three parts, the sign, its object and Gödel reading one as the other, presented as a sign referring to itself.

The proof needs Gödel at every step, to build the coding, to read the sentence as being about itself and to see that it is true, and then its conclusion is stated about the system with Gödel removed. Incompleteness belongs to that stripped object, a formal system treated as closed, and putting the checker back puts the check back, since it is how Gödel knew his sentence was true.

Notation hides the reference, and Gödel’s notation hid Gödel.

What can be proved?

There are no closed systems

A closed system cannot verify its own truth, but there are no closed systems.

Turing climbed the open tower in 1939, adding the consistency of each system to the next, and Feferman proved in 1962 that such a climb, built on reflection, reaches every true sentence of arithmetic. The path up cannot be chosen mechanically, and Turing named what the climb needs, ingenuity and intuition, the second being the step taken from outside, the checker. Tarski had already placed the truth of each language in the next one up, so every whole becomes part of something larger that can check it.

The paradoxes appear exactly where something is treated as closed: the liar, a sign pointing only at itself, which Kripke called ungrounded in 1975, Russell’s set of all sets, Burali-Forti’s greatest ordinal. So the framework predicts where paradox will appear, wherever a totality or a self-referring sign is taken as a finished thing, and that is the only place it appears.

What is matter made of?

Hamilton’s search

For more than a decade William Rowan Hamilton tried to do for space what the circle does for the plane, to find numbers whose multiplication turns directions in three dimensions, and triples of numbers would not work. On 16 October 1843, walking along the Royal Canal in Dublin, he saw that it takes four, and carved the rule into the stone of Broom Bridge.

Quaternions keep the circle inside them, since every quaternion turn is a circle about some axis, and they add what space needs, the order of the turns: turn a cup about one axis and then another, reverse the order, and it ends facing a different way, so ij = k while ji = −k.

What the notation split

Multiply two directions as quaternions and one product gives two answers at once, a number measuring how much the directions agree and a new direction perpendicular to both, pq = −p·q + p×q.

Maxwell wrote parts of his electromagnetism in quaternions, and in the 1880s Gibbs and Heaviside split the product into two notations, the dot product and the cross product, which physics still teaches separately. The split made calculation easier and hid the single operation, a turn, that both halves come from.

What is matter made of?

The tower

Doubling a number system while keeping its norm rule, where the length of a product is the product of the lengths, gives exactly four systems, the real numbers, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions, as Hurwitz proved in 1898. Each doubling gives something up: the complex numbers lose the order of the line, the quaternions lose commutativity, and the octonions lose associativity, so even the grouping of a product can change its result.

The next doubling, the sedenions, loses the norm rule itself, and two nonzero sedenions can multiply to zero. For communication this means a message and a code can combine into nothing, so decode(encode(A)) = A can no longer be guaranteed, and the tower ends exactly where the identity check stops working.

What is matter made of?

A perpendicular at every point

Every point one unit from 0 carries its own perpendicular, the circle’s tangent, and only three spheres can carry a full set of perpendicular directions at every point at once: the circle carries one, the three-dimensional sphere of the quaternions carries three, from i, j and k, and the seven-dimensional sphere of the octonions carries seven.

Bott, Milnor and Kervaire proved in 1958 that there are no others, and Adams proved in 1960 that the maps joining these spheres exist only in dimensions 1, 2, 4 and 8, the same limit the tower reaches through its algebra. The everyday version is the hairy ball: hair growing around a circle can be combed flat all the way round, and a ball of hair always leaves a tuft.

What do we measure?

The metre was a quarter turn

In 1791 the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, a quarter turn of the Earth, and the second was a fraction of the Earth’s turns, first about its axis and then around the Sun. Since 1967 the second has been 9,192,631,770 cycles of the caesium atom, a count of completed returns, and since 1983 the metre has followed from the second through the speed of light.

In 2019 every unit of the SI came to rest on fixed constants of nature that any laboratory can reproduce, which fulfils the metric system’s founding motto, for all times, for all peoples, and solves Level B for measurement with invariants anyone can recover.

The one quantity metrology still treats as a bare number is the turn itself, since the SI counts angle as dimensionless and the factor 2π between hertz and radians per second, or between h and ħ, remains a known source of error, while here the full turn is the unit that needs no outside reference.

How do minds mean?

How do we convey it?

A receiver who reads 90 as radians can receive every character and perform the wrong turn, since the transmission succeeded and the comparison did not. So how do we tell whether a person understood a relation? We ask what they can do with it: if one object is larger than another, can they recognize the reverse, smaller than, and given a second comparison, can they combine the two?

Relational Frame Theory studies exactly these, mutual entailment, combinatorial entailment and the way what a stimulus does changes through its relations. Understanding a relation is recognizing what stays the same through the right change of expression or direction, which is the identity check performed in language, visible in what the person does.
