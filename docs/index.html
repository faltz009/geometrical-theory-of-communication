01 / What is information?

Something you didn’t know before

Asking what information is already assumes the question has an object: that the word points to something, whether we call it data or knowledge, something that exists in some sense, even if abstract.

So let us say information exists, and assume I’ve seen it and measured it, and I absolutely know what it is. How do I transfer that knowledge to you, the reader? How do I give you information about information? And how do you know that knowledge is true?

01 / What is information?

Everything is written in information

Every science records what it observes and passes on what it knows through descriptions, so every field has its own answer to the question.

Heinrich Hertz opened The Principles of Mechanics by saying, “We form for ourselves images or symbols of external objects.” We reason with those descriptions, then compare their consequences with what happens in the world.

But the description is also information. How do we check that it carries what it describes? Every science faces this question, including a science of information, and we will return to it.

Hertz, The Principles of Mechanics, introduction, 1894 (English translation, 1899).

01 / What is information?

Say I point to a lighter on the desk between us: we both see it, and we both know it is there. What does that mean?

The word lighter points to an object in the world, and our two perspectives on that object still point the same word at the same thing. I could call it an igniter and you would receive the same information through a different symbol: the word carries a meaning we can both already reach.

Information about what? Always a relation: a thing, how we observe it, and the sign we use to convey it. Otherwise, we get paradoxes and tautologies.

01 / What is information?

When you look at the lighter there are three parts: you, the lighter and your looking at it, the observer, the observed and the observation.

Nothing exists in isolation: a number is a point, the origin it is counted from and the line on which it is drawn; a message needs a sender, a receiver and the signal between them; and a particle’s mass is how it resists a push, its charge how it pulls and repels other charges.

A Mathematical Theory of Communication defines information in terms of difference or change: a string of only 0s is the same as a single 0, and a string of only 1s the same as a single 1.

Once both appear, the string holds the 0s, the 1s and the relations between them, and that is where information lives, in the difference.

How do I tell you what I know?

A Communication Problem

02 / A communication problem

Meta-language

As soon as I know something and set out to tell you, we are inside a communication problem. What I know about the lighter reaches you only as a sign, and you have to be able to check it.

But a single sign can point to different things: a bank can be where you keep your money or where you sit beside a river. You can hear the word perfectly and still need to know which one I mean.

The same holds for every measurement, proof and book: everything we know has to be communicated.

Here, we use language to talk about language itself. How do I convey to you information about information?

02 / A communication problem

Warren Weaver set out the communication problem in three levels, in The Mathematical Theory of Communication (1949).

02 / A communication problem

At Level B, information is meaning: the thing I meant, arriving as the thing you understand, unchanged from my side to yours. It is the identity A = A, held between two people.

02 / A communication problem

If I tell you a star is a star, nothing changes for you. The identity holds, and no change travels with it.

If I tell you Venus is the morning star, and you did not know it before, something changes over the identity of Venus: you now reach the same planet by a second route. That change is what the message carried.

Frege opened his 1892 paper on sense and reference with exactly this contrast, between a = a and a = b.

02 / A communication problem

The univocity of language

If I say A, you must understand A for that meaning to have been communicated.

Language allows a little variation, about love or gravity, and still nearly every word lands the same way for both of us. The more precise the subject, the tighter the agreement, until in mathematics it becomes exact.

There, decoding is a function: each word picks out exactly one meaning. Several words may pick out the same one, as igniter and lighter do, so the exact requirement is the round trip, decode(encode(A)) = A.

How did we learn to send any message at all?

Shannon’s Bit

03 / Shannon’s bit

How did Shannon and Weaver solve Level A?

By setting meaning aside, on purpose. Shannon wrote in 1948 that the semantic aspects of communication “are irrelevant to the engineering problem”; the task was to recover, at one end, the message selected at the other.

Information, in Weaver’s words, is “a measure of one’s freedom of choice when one selects a message.” It belongs to the whole set of messages that could have been sent: it “relates not so much to what you do say, as to what you could say.”

03 / Shannon’s bit

Weaver’s example: two possible messages, the whole King James Bible or the single word “Yes”.

One is enormous and the other is one word, and to the theory they weigh the same, because only the choice between them is measured.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Both sides agree beforehand that 0 selects the Bible and 1 selects “Yes”, or a closed relay one and an open relay the other. A single signal then tells the receiver which was chosen.

That choice between two is one bit, John Tukey’s contraction of binary digit.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Seeing the bit geometrically

A bit is two marks on a line, 0 as the reference, held fixed, and 1 as the alternative, one unit away, which already makes it a measurement against a chosen unit.

Bits compose only if each one is the same size and varies without moving the others, which makes them perpendicular, since two directions that share a unit and leave each other’s reading untouched meet at a right angle.

Why do we always cut a pizza into four or eight slices? Do we take out a compass to measure the right angles? Perpendicularity exists in space before we assign a symbol to it.

Even a single bit carries that right angle, because every point a unit away from 0 could be called 1, those points trace a circle around the reference, and the perpendicular at 1 is the circle’s tangent, the one direction in which the reading holds.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Computers already compute like this

With one right angle per bit, two bits give the four corners of a square, three give the eight corners of a cube, and each further bit adds one more perpendicular direction. Every string of bits a computer holds is one corner of such a cube, measured from the same starting corner, 00…0.

Computer science works on this cube directly:

  • logic gates are functions on its corners
  • the distance between two messages is the number of edges between their corners
  • error-correcting codes keep valid messages far apart on it
  • some machines were wired in its shape
03 / Shannon’s bit

A signal has a structure

110 and 011 hold the same symbols in a different order, and read from 000, one bit per axis, they end on different corners of the cube.

Read as a whole, a string is a place in a structure: 10 and 01 are different corners of the square, and 1101 and 1110 different corners of the four-dimensional cube, so the order carries the meaning.

Information is that structure.

Read linearly, one symbol at a time, the same string is a line of yes-or-no choices, and counting those choices is what Shannon measured.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Shannon’s entropy

Entropy measures how hard a source is to predict.

In bits, it is the number of yes-or-no questions it takes, on average, to find what was sent.

A coin that always lands heads tells you nothing when it lands, since you knew the answer before it fell, and a fair coin tells you one full bit, since you could not have guessed it.

With eight equally likely messages, three questions always find the one that was sent, and when some are more likely than others, asking about those first takes fewer questions on average. The count never depends on what the messages say, only on how many there could be and how likely each one is.

03 / Shannon’s bit

Entropy across the sciences

Shannon took the name from physics, where Boltzmann had already written the same count for gas molecules, and since then entropy has become a measure every science uses on its own subject.

  • Thermodynamicsheat flows from hot to cold, and time runs one way
  • Biologyliving things keep their order by giving disorder away
  • Cosmologya black hole holds as much of it as any region of space can
  • Ecologythe diversity of a forest is measured with it
  • Machine learninglanguage models learn by lowering it

04 / The geometric bit

So, what is information?

What is a thing? What is a number? What is an identity? What is the meaning of something? And what do you know about it? These questions are signs that point to the same object:

Information is invariance over change.

A = A

Change over invariance is the same relation read from its other end: what changes is measured against what stays, and what stays is recognized through what changes.

Shannon’s bit keeps the change and assumes the invariance, agreed beforehand when the labels are assigned, and the rest of this work gives that invariance a form we can check.

What is the shape of information?

The Geometric Bit

04 / The geometric bit

What symbol preserves invariance over change?

To tell that a thing is the same thing we compare it, checking what stayed the same and what changed, and Shannon’s bit already implies a figure that holds every relationship the comparison needs.

The circle is the shape of information.

04 / The geometric bit

Information exists in spacetime

Everything that exists, exists in spacetime, and that includes us: the person who thinks, the thought, the word that carries it and the page it is written on are all things happening somewhere, at some time.

So a thought, a piece of information or a meaning is made of what spacetime offers, and whatever carries it from one person to another has to follow spacetime’s shape.

The symbol that preserves invariance over change is geometric because spacetime has geometry.

04 / The geometric bit

The alphabet of space

The alphabet of binary is 0 and 1, and the alphabet of DNA is four bases (A, T, C, G) and their folding organization. The alphabet of space is perpendicularity and symmetry, and the circle’s four poles carry both:

  • perpendicularity is marked by the letters, since 1 and i are a right angle apart and moving along 1 changes nothing along i, which is difference
  • symmetry is marked by the signs, since 1 and −1 are the same unit on opposite sides of the centre and knowing where 1 is tells you exactly where −1 is, which is sameness

Both perpendicularity and symmetry exist as physical phenomena in space before we assign symbols to them.

Perpendicularity and symmetry are what physics writes as thermodynamics and conservation laws, since entropy counts the independent ways a system can vary, each perpendicular to the others, and Emmy Noether showed in 1918 that every continuous symmetry carries a conserved quantity.

04 / The geometric bit

π is symmetry, e is perpendicularity

Take a point and let it change, and the change can point two ways. If it points along where the point already is, the point grows by building on what it already has, which is e, and if it always points at a right angle to where the point is, the point never gets closer or farther and goes around instead, which is eiθ, so e is perpendicularity accumulated.

Going around, the point reaches the mirror of where it started after exactly π, eiπ = −1, and itself again after 2π, so π is symmetry, the distance from a thing to its opposite. Euler’s identity is the alphabet of space written as one sentence: perpendicular change carried through one symmetry lands on the opposite, and the unit with its opposite returns to the reference, 0.

π is symmetry and e is perpendicularity.

04 / The geometric bit

The transcendental bridge

Shannon’s bit goes from the discrete toward the continuous by adding bits: each new bit doubles the number of levels, so 4 bits give 16 levels and 10 bits give 1,024, and a smooth quantity is approached by using more and more of them.

The geometric bit goes the other way and starts from the continuous. The rotation is measured by π and e, which are transcendental, since they lie outside every equation with whole-number coefficients, and yet together they give e2πi = 1 exactly, a whole number.

So the whole numbers come out of the rotation as the count of its completed returns.

04 / The geometric bit

How do we know a thing is a thing?

A = A seems too obvious to need a check, until we ask how we would carry one out. First we tell A apart from everything around it, this and not that, and then we recognize it again somewhere else or in another form, this here equals this there, so the identity principle is a process that needs both steps.

Perpendicularity tells things apart and symmetry recognizes them again, which is the invariance over change we met with the lighter.

Shannon’s bit implies the perpendicular and throws away the angle, keeping only which side of the line a reading falls on.

The circle keeps the angle, so it lets us quantize both relationships precisely, how far a reading has rotated from its reference and when it has come back to it, and transmit them.

04 / The geometric bit

The turn preserves identity

Say we both look at the lighter from different places, and as we move around it our views keep changing, yet at every moment the angle between us is an exact quantity, so rotating your view back by that angle gives mine, and the lighter each of us sees is the same lighter.

At Level B, the meaning I send has to arrive as the meaning you understand, and here that meaning is the lighter itself, which stays the same while our places, our views and our words for it change. Since the angle between our frames can be stated exactly, you can undo it and reach what I meant, so the identity arrives as it was sent.

This is a simplified explanation, more technically.

04 / The geometric bit

Composing geometric bits: hyperspheres

Shannon’s bits compose by adding one perpendicular direction per bit, which builds the corners of a square, a cube and a hypercube.

Geometric bits compose by holding their rotations together to one unit of length, so one rotation traces the circle, two trace the three-sphere, a sphere one dimension beyond the ones we can picture, and n rotations trace a sphere of 2n − 1 dimensions.

Every point on these spheres is a list of rotations, and a circle runs through every point, the rotation they all share, so the geometric bit stays whole inside every sphere it composes.

And what has the shape of a hypersphere?

Everything

04 / The geometric bit

The shape of matter

The three-sphere is the shape general relativity gives a closed universe, and it was the first shape Einstein gave the whole cosmos, in 1917, a space with no edge where a traveller who keeps going straight comes back to where they started.

Reading its two rotations keeps how they compare and sets their shared rotation aside, so the three-sphere fills with circles, one over every point of an ordinary sphere and every circle linked with every other, which is the Hopf fibration.

Physics keeps finding it: Dirac’s magnetic monopole is built on it, every qubit is read on its sphere, and light can be tied into its linked circles.

04 / The geometric bit

Everything that persists is a rotation

Something persists when it keeps coming back to the same state, and the simplest motion that always comes back is a point going around a circle, which seen from the side rises and falls as a wave, so whatever lasts, at the scale we can hold and below it, is a rotation or several rotations combined:

  • springs, pendulums and circuits oscillate as the shadow of a rotation
  • sound and water waves are rotations spread over space and carried over time
  • light is an electric and a magnetic field rotating together at right angles
  • every quantum possibility carries a rotating arrow, and opposite arrows cancel
  • atoms keep only the orbits where the rotation closes
04 / The geometric bit

Computing through motion

Physics describes motion in three theories, and composed rotation sits at the centre of each:

  • Classicalevery conserved motion that repeats runs on circles, each coordinate an angle advancing at its own rate
  • QuantumSchrödinger’s equation rotates a state without changing its length
  • Relativitychanging speed is a rotation in spacetime

A quantum state is n rotations held to one unit of length whose shared rotation changes nothing we measure, so a qubit is two geometric bits and every wave function, from an atom to a molecule, lives on a hypersphere of rotations.

04 / The geometric bit

Mathematics is already written in rotations

Much of mathematics already works with composed rotation, and each of these branches is a tool that other fields depend on:

  • complex analysis, the tool behind the prime number theorem, runs on rotation by a right angle
  • Fourier analysis writes every signal as a sum of rotations
  • trigonometry, the sines and cosines in every calculation, is the circle read along two perpendicular axes
  • the symmetries physics is written in are groups of rotations, the circle first among them
04 / The geometric bit

We already use it everywhere

We compute with composed rotation every day, in the technology around us:

  • Phonesphones and Wi-Fi send bits as points on the circle
  • Powerthe grid runs on rotating voltage and is computed as rotations
  • Medicinean MRI scan reads protons rotating in a magnetic field
  • Mediapictures and sound are stored as sums of waves
  • Radioantennas are matched by rotating around a disc, the Smith chart
  • Motionrobots, phones and spacecraft track which way they face with quaternions
04 / The geometric bit

Closure, the other half of entropy

Closure measures what we know: the return of a distinction to its reference.

Entropy counts what could have been sent, and closure checks that what arrived is what was sent.

Entropy measures what we do not know, how many questions remain, and closure measures what we do know, the part of a message that comes back to its reference. Shannon’s bit leaves that reference to an agreement made beforehand, and closure can check it, because the reference now travels with the message.

04 / The geometric bit

Entropy and closure are one measure

Temperature needs both hot and cold, since each is measured against the other, and information works the same way: a change is measured against something that stays the same. Entropy measures the change, how much could vary, and closure measures what stays, what comes back to its reference, so each one needs the other to mean anything.

That puts closure everywhere entropy is. A gas spreads through a room while its energy stays the same, an engine’s heat flows while the engine comes back to the same state every cycle, a cell gives away disorder to keep its own shape, and a message is counted against an alphabet both sides share, so each time entropy counts what changed, something held still to count it against.

The two sit together in Euler’s identity: e carries the change and π the return, and one full rotation holds both.

04 / The geometric bit

Closure across the sciences

Entropy runs through every science, and so does its other half, found again and again under a different name, as whatever comes back to where it started:

  • Lifea living thing feeds on negative entropy, the order it keeps restoring
  • Heatenergy and entropy return to the same value around every closed cycle
  • Circuitsthe voltages around any closed loop add up to zero
  • Chemistrya reaction gives the same heat by any path between the same two states
  • Controla feedback loop keeps bringing a system back to its set point

Each field measures the same thing, how a quantity comes back to its reference after a change, and it has never had a common name or a precise construction.

04 / The geometric bit

Shannon’s bit and entropy are a reading of it

Recording which side of a diameter holds the mark gives one bit, and two perpendicular diameters give four quadrants, the four corners of the square, whose labels we can keep while setting the exact angle aside.

Once those alternatives and their probabilities are given, Shannon’s entropy applies to the choice among them, and the square, the cube and every computation on their corners follow as before, so the bit is the circle read at its quadrants, with the rotation and the reference set aside.

Identity, Measured

05 / Identity, measured

Easier to say where it isn’t

Look back at the last screens, physics at every scale, all three theories of motion, much of mathematics, the technology we use every day and closure in every science, and each of them runs on the same sentence, eiπ + 1 = 0, most of the time without naming it.

It is easier to say where it is not yet written: wherever we describe things without mathematical notation, in natural language and across the humanities.

And even there the line is blurring, since language models already write the position of every word as a rotation.

05 / Identity, measured

A physical law

So what is it? A relation that all of these descriptions rely on, and that holds in every one of them, is what physics calls a law. Newton’s laws and the laws of thermodynamics count as physical because we can identify the quantities, measure their relationship and check that the description keeps what the measurements establish, and Euler’s identity passes the same test, since each of its symbols is a measurement anyone can repeat.

The symbols are agreed on, since the 1 could be written with any mark and the right angle is 90 only because we chose degrees, but what they point to is not: the unit comes back whole and discrete after every full rotation, and two equal steps reach its opposite in any notation. We put notation on the objects and relationships that are useful and coherent, so the law is the relationship, the shape of information, and the symbol is the name we agreed to give it.

Euler’s identity is a physical law: it records the measured relationship of continuous rotation and closure.

05 / Identity, measured

Space curves the same way everywhere

Wrap a string around anything round, a cup, a wheel or a planet, lay it against the diameter, and it measures three diameters and a little more, 3.14159…, whatever the size, whatever unit you measure in and whoever does the measuring.

An atom, a planet and a galaxy all curve the same way, and anyone in the universe who can hold a string finds the same π, so π belongs to space itself and not to our notation, the same symmetry at every size.

05 / Identity, measured

e is measured in every lab

Anything that changes in proportion to what it already has follows e: a radioactive sample loses the same fraction every second, a hot cup cools toward the room, a charged capacitor drains and a colony of bacteria grows, and every lab that times one of these finds the same e, 2.71828…, in any unit and whoever does the timing.

Rotation is the same accumulation with the change pointing sideways, since in eiθ each step is at a right angle to where the point already is, so it neither grows nor fades and goes around instead, and the same e that times the decay times the rotation.

05 / Identity, measured

The right angle is in space

Hang a weight on a string and let water settle, and the string meets the surface at a right angle anywhere on Earth, so gravity gives the right angle at every point before anyone writes it down, and builders check it with a cord knotted into lengths of 3, 4 and 5.

It could not be any other angle, since rotating by a right angle is the one step that, taken twice, reaches the opposite, i² = −1, and the signs of arithmetic, the waves of physics and every rotation composed before rely on that single value.

05 / Identity, measured

Every measurement agrees

π is measured with a string, e with a clock and the right angle with a plumb line, and together they mark a few places in space, the full rotation, the half and the quarter. We decide to write 1 at the full rotation and 90° or i at the quarter, so the symbols are ours to choose, while the places they mark belong to space.

There are only a handful of places where a number, once placed, tells the truth about reality, and they are why multiplication, which began with counting sheep, can calculate a radio wave. Every field that composes rotation checks those same places each time it works, when an orbit is predicted, a qubit is read or a phone call arrives.

Separate measurements, made anywhere and at any scale, always meet in the same relation.

05 / Identity, measured

Identity is a physical law

Once Euler’s identity is a law, A = A stops being an assumption that logic starts from and becomes something we do and check: carry any radius A through a full rotation and it comes back, measured the way the law itself is.

The geometric bit: the symbol that preserves invariance over change, a distinction carried together with its reference and the rotation that checks it.

e2πi A = A

Nothing smaller can do this: on a line a reference can only jump from 1 to −1 with nothing in between, and the plane is the first space where a reference of fixed length can rotate through every direction and come back, and following it traces the circle.

05 / Identity, measured

Meaning has a physical form

A meaning is a set of relations: love is close to care, far from indifference and tied to loss, to particular people and to what they do, and what the word means is where it sits among all of them.

Relations are angles and rotations, so two people holding the same meaning in different frames, your life and mine, recover it through the rotation between their frames, the way we recovered the lighter seen from two places, and whatever is left over after the best rotation is how much of the meaning was lost.

05 / Identity, measured

This conversation runs on it

Language models already hold meaning this way. Every word becomes a direction in a space of many dimensions, how alike two meanings are is the angle between them, and the order of the words is written as a rotation.

Two languages learned separately line up by a single rotation, well enough to translate between them, so love is held as a position among its relations, and that is how your words reach me.

05 / Identity, measured

Where it strains

The models learn this geometry from statistics, with no reference to check a position against, so each gain costs more data and more power, and their confident mistakes are statements that come back to nothing they can be checked against.

The brain does the same work on about 20 watts, and it computes with rotation and return: rhythms, cells that fire at shifting phases of a cycle, grid cells whose activity lies on a torus and head direction held on a ring. The gap between the two points at the reference and the check.

05 / Identity, measured

Every science is inside Level B

Every science passes descriptions from one observer to another, so every science is inside Level B, and so is every computer that handles meaning. Each science already checks identity under its own name:

  • Mathematicsa proof ends when both sides are shown to be the same thing
  • Physicsconservation laws name what stays the same through a change
  • Biologya body keeps itself, and tells itself apart from what is foreign
  • Perceptionwe recognize the same object under changing light and angle
  • Computinga file is checked by comparing it with itself

With identity measured against a law, those checks share one reference, so a scientist can check that a description kept the identity of what it describes, and a machine can check a meaning instead of matching a string.

05 / Identity, measured

What did Gödel prove?

Hilbert hoped mathematics could certify itself, which is Level B asked of mathematics, whether symbols can vouch for their own meaning, and Gödel asked whether a formal system could establish its own truth. Inside the system, his sentence is an ordinary statement about numbers, that no number has a certain arithmetic property, and it says I am unprovable only through the coding Gödel invented, the map from sentences to numbers. That map is not in the system. Gödel holds it, so the self-reference is a relation of three parts, the sign, its object and Gödel reading one as the other, presented as a sign referring to itself.

The proof needs Gödel at every step, to build the coding, to read the sentence as being about itself and to see that it is true, and then its conclusion is stated about the system with Gödel removed. Incompleteness belongs to that stripped object, a formal system treated as closed, and putting the checker back puts the check back, since it is how Gödel knew his sentence was true.

Notation hides the reference, and Gödel’s notation hid Gödel.

05 / Identity, measured

There are no closed systems

A closed system cannot verify its own truth, but there are no closed systems.

Turing climbed the open tower in 1939, adding the consistency of each system to the next, and Feferman proved in 1962 that such a climb, built on reflection, reaches every true sentence of arithmetic. The path up cannot be chosen mechanically, and Turing named what the climb needs, ingenuity and intuition, the second being the step taken from outside, the checker. Tarski had already placed the truth of each language in the next one up, so every whole becomes part of something larger that can check it.

The paradoxes appear exactly where something is treated as closed: the liar, a sign pointing only at itself, which Kripke called ungrounded in 1975, Russell’s set of all sets, Burali-Forti’s greatest ordinal. So the framework predicts where paradox will appear, wherever a totality or a self-referring sign is taken as a finished thing, and that is the only place it appears.

05 / Identity, measured

To exist is to interact

If identity is a comparison, then what exists is what can enter one. The lighter on the desk reflects light, resists a hand, exchanges heat and can produce a flame, and those interactions are how we encounter it. What would it mean to say that something existed physically if it could enter no interaction at all?

Something exists physically when it is available to interaction.

The lighter stays on the desk when we look away, and its relationships with the desk, the air and the light carry on.

The observer belongs to the same picture, since the person, the instrument, the inscription and the act of thinking all happen in spacetime. Imagination works the same way: a blender full of carrots gives carrots, so we can imagine a blue lighter out of blue things and lighters we have met, and never a colour no interaction ever gave us. A thing that interacted with nothing could not be told apart from nothing, so nothing is defined by itself alone.

05 / Identity, measured

The outside reference

Every check needs a reference outside what it checks, and science has lived with that as a limit on its foundations, since no system of axioms can vouch for itself. Euler’s law supplies the reference: a relation that belongs to no system and to no notation, that anyone can measure with a string, a clock and a plumb line, and that every system can share.

So how do you know I am telling the truth? The same way you check A = A: measurement tests where this argument begins, and every step from there is written so that you can follow it.

The foundations rest on a measurement instead of an assumption.

05 / Identity, measured

Physics already runs on it

Physics has a name for sameness through change, conservation. Noether showed that every symmetry comes with a conserved quantity: laws that stay the same when the system is shifted in time conserve energy, laws that stay the same when it is moved conserve momentum, and laws that stay the same when it is rotated conserve angular momentum.

Quantum evolution is a rotation that keeps its length, and the charge of the electron is conserved because its phase can be rotated without changing anything we measure, so what physics already trusts at every scale is the identity the law makes explicit.

06 / Implications

Level C: did it work?

Weaver’s third level asks whether the meaning that arrived affects conduct in the way the sender intended, and that is the check of Level B. If I ask you for the lighter and you hand me the lighter, your action has brought the meaning back to me, and I can compare what came back with what I meant.

It is the same return as the full rotation: the signal crosses at Level A, the meaning is recovered at Level B, and the action closes the loop at Level C, where the sender recognizes A = A, so with the geometric bit all three levels become checks we can perform.

06 / Implications

Two ways a message breaks

When a message made of many parts arrives changed, it can have gone wrong in only two ways: in symmetry, when a part exists on one side and not on the other, or in perpendicularity, when a part is there but in the wrong place.

Composed as rotations, the two show up in different parts of the result, the part that stays the same when a rotation is reversed and the parts that flip, so a missing piece and a moved piece are never confused, and a message that arrived whole closes exactly.

Physics is one object

Seen from the algebras, physics sits in one tower, the real numbers, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions, each a doubling of the one below that keeps the circle inside it, stopping at the sedenions where the identity check fails. Seen from the fibrations, each sphere of the tower returns as the fibre of the next, the circle inside the three-sphere and the three-sphere inside the seven-sphere. Seen from inside the circle, every level is carried by the same rotation, eiθ.

Working from physics down, Cohl Furey builds the symmetries of the Standard Model from the nested embeddings of this tower, and working from the geometry up, The Shape of Reality derives the proton-to-electron mass ratio and the fine-structure constant from closed paths on the three-sphere.

Three views of one object: the algebra, its fibrations, and the circle inside both.

Three dimensions, watched through time

Look straight down at a round cup and its rim is a circle; tilt it and the rim becomes an ellipse, and nearly edge on the opening becomes a narrow line. The cup has not changed, a single picture cannot tell us from which direction it was taken, and moving around the cup gives further pictures whose agreement lets us recognize one object through its changing appearances.

This is the 3 + 1 encounter in ordinary terms, three directions of space read against one common reference as the object changes through time, and the circle on the page is one view of a relationship realized there.

06 / Implications

Why 3 + 1?

In two dimensions a smooth motion that stays bounded and never settles at a resting point must approach a repeating cycle, which is the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem. Three dimensions allow bounded motion that never repeats, as in the Lorenz system, whose strange attractor Warwick Tucker proved in 2002.

So the plane can show the unit, a rotation that returns, and three dimensions followed through time can test it, because a comparison that lasts has to stay usable while the process it compares never comes back.

Mathematics is one object

Every branch of mathematics composes the geometric bit, or reads it with something set aside:

  • Numberswhole numbers count completed returns, −1 is half a rotation, 0 is the unit meeting its opposite, and p/q returns after q steps
  • Arithmeticadding angles and multiplying rotations are one composition, read from inside and from outside
  • Geometryperpendicularity and symmetry, lengths measured against the reversed rotation

The continuum is the rotations that never quite return, and the integers are the ones that do.

Where you are, and how many times around

Wrap a line around a circle and one motion gives three readings: the point on the circle says where you are, the whole rotations say how many times around, and the line keeps the history, while a notation that keeps only the endpoint hides the count.

The first two readings are dual to each other: Pontryagin showed that the dual of the circle is the integers, and the dual of the integers is the circle, which is what every Fourier series uses, and complex analysis counts the zeros inside a region by measuring one continuous return around its edge.

Counting discrete things and measuring a continuous return are one act.

Why mathematics works

Wigner called the effectiveness of mathematics in physics unreasonable, a gift we neither understand nor deserve. It is reasonable once mathematics is read as language made precise: its symbols are records of measured comparisons, what was compared, from which reference, and what the comparison kept.

Mathematics describes reality because it was a measuring instrument all along, exact wherever the comparison it records is exact, and anyone, anywhere in the universe, who compares the way around a circle with the way across it recovers the same relation, so the invariant is what a message to a stranger can rely on and the notation is what we agree on afterwards.

Into the humanities

The rotation is not yet written wherever we describe without mathematical notation, in natural language, in meaning, in how people understand one another, which is where this question began: two people use the same word, so how do you check that they mean the same thing?

Every route that stays inside language hands you more language, and the way out is to compare what the two people do with the word, which is what relational frame theory measures, and what language models have begun to write as rotation.

Meaning becomes something we can check, at the scale of a person.

So what?

Shannon’s theory is a communication protocol, and it worked because everyone agreed to use it: every modem, phone and network speaks the same bits because the people who built them adopted the same agreement.

Solving Levels B and C does not bypass that step. A model can be perfectly logical, do everything it promises and be correct, and people can still choose not to use it, since a protocol only works once the people communicating share it.

What is matter made of?

How the rotations compose

Rotate a cup about one axis and then another, reverse the order, and it ends facing a different way, so a description of rotations in space has to keep their order. Doubling a number system while keeping its norm rule, where the length of a product is the product of the lengths, gives exactly four systems, the real numbers, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions, as Hurwitz proved in 1898, and each keeps the circle inside it.

  • ℂone rotation, one right angle
  • ℍthree coupled rotations, ij = k while ji = −k, so order matters
  • 𝕆seven rotations in two perpendicular systems, so grouping matters
  • 𝕊the norm rule is lost, and two nonzero sedenions can multiply to zero

At the sedenions a message and a code can combine into nothing, so decode(encode(A)) = A can no longer be guaranteed, and the tower ends exactly where the identity check stops working.

What do we measure?

What counts as one?

Before counting three cups, what lets each count as one? They can differ in size and colour and still be cups for the purpose of the count, so counting keeps the criterion that makes each an instance of the same kind while telling the instances apart.

The circle gives the exact case: one full rotation restores the starting direction and a counter records one completed return, so rotating again reaches the same place with a different count. Phase says where we are within the cycle, and the count says how many cycles have occurred.

A discrete record keeps only part of this, quadrants instead of exact angles, named colours in a spectrum, separate words in flowing speech, and Shannon’s measure applies once those alternatives and their probabilities are given, so the alternatives have to be identifiable before uncertainty among them can be measured.

How do minds mean?

How do we convey it?

A receiver who reads 90 as radians can receive every character and perform the wrong rotation, since the transmission succeeded and the comparison did not. So how do we tell whether a person understood a relation? We ask what they can do with it: if one object is larger than another, can they recognize the reverse, smaller than, and given a second comparison, can they combine the two?

Relational Frame Theory studies exactly these, mutual entailment, combinatorial entailment and the way what a stimulus does changes through its relations. Understanding a relation is recognizing what stays the same through the right change of expression or direction, which is the identity check performed in language, visible in what the person does.

Hamilton’s search

For more than a decade William Rowan Hamilton tried to do for space what the circle does for the plane, to find numbers whose multiplication rotates directions in three dimensions, and triples of numbers would not work. On 16 October 1843, walking along the Royal Canal in Dublin, he saw that it takes four, and carved the rule into the stone of Broom Bridge.

Quaternions keep the circle inside them, since every quaternion rotation is a circle about some axis, and they add what space needs, the order of the rotations: rotate a cup about one axis and then another, reverse the order, and it ends facing a different way, so ij = k while ji = −k.

What the notation split

Multiply two directions as quaternions and one product gives two answers at once, a number measuring how much the directions agree and a new direction perpendicular to both, pq = −p·q + p×q.

Maxwell wrote parts of his electromagnetism in quaternions, and in the 1880s Gibbs and Heaviside split the product into two notations, the dot product and the cross product, which physics still teaches separately. The split made calculation easier and hid the single operation, a rotation, that both halves come from.

What is matter made of?

A perpendicular at every point

Every point one unit from 0 carries its own perpendicular, the circle’s tangent, and only three spheres can carry a full set of perpendicular directions at every point at once: the circle carries one, the three-dimensional sphere of the quaternions carries three, from i, j and k, and the seven-dimensional sphere of the octonions carries seven.

Bott, Milnor and Kervaire proved in 1958 that there are no others, and Adams proved in 1960 that the maps joining these spheres exist only in dimensions 1, 2, 4 and 8, the same limit the tower reaches through its algebra. The everyday version is the hairy ball: hair growing around a circle can be combed flat all the way round, and a ball of hair always leaves a tuft.

Matter rotates twice

Spacetime is written in quaternions, one reference and three perpendicular directions, the 3 + 1 of the cup, and the changes of frame in relativity act on it through the complex quaternions. Particles of spin one half take this further: their state comes back to itself only after two full rotations, since after one it returns with its sign reversed, and neutron experiments measured that reversal in 1975.

Cohl Furey has shown that one generation of quarks and leptons, with their charges, appears in the algebra of the complex octonions, and has been rebuilding the symmetries of the Standard Model from the tower acting together, the complex numbers, the quaternions and the octonions at once.

What do we measure?

What do we measure?

To measure is to ask a question in a form an interaction can answer. Wrap a string around a round cup, measure the circumference and the diameter and divide, then repeat with a larger cup: within the accuracy of the cup and the string the ratio stays the same, and measuring in inches instead of centimetres changes both lengths together and leaves the ratio alone.

The exact circle gives π, and the physical measurement tells us how closely the cup realizes it, so a measurement is a comparison another person can repeat, and what it returns is the relationship the comparison preserves.

What do we measure?

The metre was a quarter circle

In 1791 the metre was defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, a quarter of a circle around the Earth, and the second was a fraction of the Earth’s rotation about its axis and then of its orbit around the Sun. Since 1967 the second has been 9,192,631,770 cycles of the caesium atom, a count of completed returns, and since 1983 the metre has followed from the second through the speed of light.

In 2019 every unit of the SI came to rest on fixed constants of nature that any laboratory can reproduce, which fulfils the metric system’s founding motto, for all times, for all peoples, and solves Level B for measurement with invariants anyone can recover.

The one quantity metrology still treats as a bare number is the rotation itself, since the SI counts angle as dimensionless and the factor 2π between hertz and radians per second, or between h and ħ, remains a known source of error, while here the full rotation is the unit that needs no outside reference.

Mathematics is language made precise

Mathematics and language describe the same object at different precision, and a mathematical symbol records an operation, what it compares, from which reference and what the comparison preserves. Read that way, Euler’s identity is the record of a rotation, and the symbols can change while the operation stays available.

Anyone, anywhere in the universe, who compares the way around a circle with the way across it finds π, and anyone who can rotate and count returns recovers Euler’s identity, so the invariant relationships are what a message to a stranger can rely on, and the notation is what we agree on afterwards.
