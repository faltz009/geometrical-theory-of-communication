What is information?

01 / The question

Something you didn’t know before

Asking what information is already assumes the question has an object: that the word points to something, whether we call it data or knowledge, something that exists in some sense, even if abstract.

Let us say it does, and let us say that I, the author, have seen it and measured it, and so I know what information is. How do I transfer that knowledge to you, the reader? How do I give you information about information? And how do you know that knowledge is true?

01 / The question

Everything is written in information

Every science records what it observes and passes on what it knows through descriptions, so every field has its own answer to the question.

Each answer works inside its field, and what the answers share is hard to see from any one of them.

02 / One relation

Say I point to a lighter on the desk between us: we both see it, and we both know it is there. What does that mean?

The word lighter points to an object in the world, and our two perspectives on that object still point the same word at the same thing. I could call it an igniter and you would receive the same information through a different symbol: the word carries a meaning we can both already reach.

Information about what? Always about a thing, and always carried by a sign.

02 / One relation

Through all of that, one thing holds: the lighter stays the same while the views, the words and the people change.

Information is invariance over change.

A = A

It is a process: from the thing, to a sign, and back.

  • meaningwhat the sign carries
  • identitywhat the thing keeps
  • informationwhat travels between them
  • knowledgethe return that checks it
02 / One relation

The object, our view of it and the symbol for it separate three questions:

What exists?How do we observe it, and know it?How do we represent it, and pass it on?

Each field answers one of them from its own place on the relation, which is why, from inside any one field, the others look like separate subjects.

03 / A communication problem

As soon as I know something and set out to tell you, we are inside a communication problem. What I know about the lighter reaches you only as a sign, and you have to be able to check it.

The same holds for every measurement, proof and book: everything we know has to be communicated.

03 / A communication problem

Warren Weaver set out the communication problem in three levels, in The Mathematical Theory of Communication (1949).

03 / A communication problem

At Level B, information is meaning: the thing I meant, arriving as the thing you understand, unchanged from my side to yours. It is the identity A = A, held between two people.

03 / A communication problem

If I tell you a star is a star, nothing changes for you. The identity holds, and no change travels with it.

If I tell you Venus is the morning star, and you did not know it before, something changes over the identity of Venus: you now reach the same planet by a second route. That change is what the message carried.

Frege opened his 1892 paper on sense and reference with exactly this contrast, between a = a and a = b.

03 / A communication problem

The univocity of language

If I say A, you must understand A for that meaning to have been communicated.

Language allows a little variation, about love or gravity, and still nearly every word lands the same way for both of us. The more precise the subject, the tighter the agreement, until in mathematics it becomes exact.

There, decoding is a function: each word picks out exactly one meaning. Several words may pick out the same one, as igniter and lighter do, so the exact requirement is the round trip, decode(encode(A)) = A.

04 / Shannon’s bit

How did Shannon and Weaver solve Level A?

By setting meaning aside, on purpose. Shannon wrote in 1948 that the semantic aspects of communication “are irrelevant to the engineering problem”; the task was to recover, at one end, the message selected at the other.

Information, in Weaver’s words, is “a measure of one’s freedom of choice when one selects a message.” It belongs to the whole set of messages that could have been sent: it “relates not so much to what you do say, as to what you could say.”

04 / Shannon’s bit

Weaver’s example: two possible messages, the whole King James Bible or the single word “Yes”.

One is enormous and the other is one word, and to the theory they weigh the same, because only the choice between them is measured.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Both sides agree beforehand that 0 selects the Bible and 1 selects “Yes”, or a closed relay one and an open relay the other. A single signal then tells the receiver which was chosen.

That choice between two is one bit, John Tukey’s contraction of binary digit.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Seeing the bit geometrically

A bit is two marks on a line, 0 as the reference, held fixed, and 1 as the alternative, one unit away, which already makes it a measurement against a chosen unit.

Bits compose only if each one is the same size and varies without moving the others, which makes them perpendicular, since two directions that share a unit and leave each other’s reading untouched meet at a right angle.

Why do we always cut a pizza into four or eight slices? Do we take out a compass to measure the right angles? Perpendicularity exists in space before we assign a symbol to it.

Even a single bit carries that right angle, because every point a unit away from 0 could be called 1, those points trace a circle around the reference, and the perpendicular at 1 is the circle’s tangent, the one direction in which the reading holds.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Computers already compute like this

With one right angle per bit, two bits give the four corners of a square, three give the eight corners of a cube, and each further bit adds one more perpendicular direction. Every string of bits a computer holds is one corner of such a cube, measured from the same starting corner, 00…0.

Computer science works on this cube directly:

  • logic gates are functions on its corners
  • the distance between two messages is the number of edges between their corners
  • error-correcting codes keep valid messages far apart on it
  • some machines were wired in its shape
04 / Shannon’s bit

Shannon’s entropy

Entropy is the number of yes-or-no questions it takes, on average, to find out which message was sent.

Each question is one bit, answered 0 or 1.

With eight equally likely messages, three questions always find the one that was sent, each answer halving what remains, and the three answers together spell its corner of the cube. When some messages are more likely than others we can ask about those first, so a predictable source needs fewer questions on average and a surprising one needs more.

04 / Shannon’s bit

Entropy across the sciences

Entropy counts the ways a thing can be arranged while looking the same from outside.

The messages that could have been sent are one case of those arrangements. A drop of ink spreads through water and never gathers back because far more arrangements of its molecules look spread than gathered, and fields that give different answers to what information is all use this same count.

  • heat flows from hot to cold, and time runs one way
  • living things keep their order by giving disorder away
  • a black hole holds as much of it as any region of space can
  • ecologists measure the diversity of a forest with it
  • language models learn by lowering it
04 / Shannon’s bit

What the bit gives us

  • encodesa difference, 0 or 1, measured from a fixed reference
  • composesone perpendicular direction per bit, into squares, cubes and hypercubes
  • gives usclassical information theory, digital computation, and every machine built on discrete states
  • assumesthat both sides already agree on what each corner selects

Every transmitted bit therefore depends on two checks: the receiver has to recognize the state that arrived as the one that was sent, which Shannon solved, and both sides have to agree on what that state selects, which is Level B. How can that agreement become something we perform and check, instead of something we assume when we assign the labels?

05 / The geometric bit

What symbol preserves invariance over change?

To tell that a thing is the same thing we compare it, checking what stayed the same and what changed, and Shannon’s bit already implies a figure that holds every relationship the comparison needs.

The circle is the shape of information.

05 / The geometric bit

Drawing the perpendicular

Any point one unit from 0 could serve as 1, and all of them together make a circle around 0. The perpendicular the bit left undrawn stands at each of those points as the circle’s tangent, the direction in which that point’s reading holds.

If we mark a starting point and follow the circle round, the mark changes position the whole way and after a full turn is back at the start, where we can compare the two. That change, which returns what it started from and lets us check the return, is the unit we identify as the geometric bit.

05 / The geometric bit

The alphabet of space

The alphabet of binary is 0 and 1, and the alphabet of DNA is four bases. The alphabet of space is perpendicularity and symmetry, and the circle’s four poles carry both, since 1 and −1 are symmetric, the same unit on opposite sides of the centre, and 1 and i are perpendicular, a quarter turn apart.

Perpendicularity separates two changes, which is difference, and symmetry keeps every length and right angle while the whole circle turns, which is sameness.

Both perpendicularity and symmetry exist as physical phenomena in space before we assign symbols to them.

Physics writes both down as law, since entropy counts the ways a system can be arranged, each independent two-state part doubling them, and Emmy Noether showed in 1918 that every continuous symmetry carries a conserved quantity, so turning gives angular momentum and the passing of time gives energy.

05 / The geometric bit

Two references, one turn

Put your reference at the top of the circle while I put mine on the right, and let us both make a quarter turn in the same direction. Our marks finish in different places, yet each has turned through the same angle from its own reference, because turning a reference together with its reading adds the same angle to both and leaves their difference fixed.

Your circle can also be larger than mine: circumference and diameter grow together, keeping the ratio π, while the right angles and the fraction of a turn stay the same, so what we share is the comparison we perform, whatever drawing each of us holds.

05 / The geometric bit

Counting turns

Let 1 mark the starting radius and let multiplication by i make a positive quarter turn. Two turns reach the opposite pole, so i² = −1, the third reaches −i, and the fourth returns to 1, where the count carries on, so i4 and i8 land where i0 began and every power of i lands on one of the four poles.

One movement then gives two readings, where the point is and how many turns it has made, the first continuous and the second a count of completed returns. Multiplication and addition set how the poles combine, since multiplying by i adds a quarter turn and adding two perpendicular poles, 1 + i, reaches the corner of the square between them.

05 / The geometric bit

Euler writes it down

Euler’s formula eiθ follows the turn through every angle between the poles, and at the half turn and the full turn it reads eiπ + 1 = 0 and e2πi = 1, the first recording opposition as cancellation and the second the completed return.

Its π measures the turn in space and its e the accumulation in time, so at a steady rate the angle grows with elapsed time, θ = ωt, and each completed return is an event we can count. Classical mechanics, quantum mechanics and relativity describe their oscillations, phases and rotations with it.

05 / The geometric bit

On the circle, the agreement Shannon’s bit assumes becomes a comparison both sides perform, because the reference, the turn and the check that recognizes the result travel together.

The geometric bit: the symbol that preserves invariance over change, a distinction carried together with its reference and the turn that checks it.

e2πi A = A

Starting from any radius A, the full turn returns it, so A = A becomes something we do.

You can draw the two perpendicular diameters while I follow four quarter turns, and we can compare where the poles lie, the angles between them and the return to the start. The drawing and the formula lead to the same checks, so what we understood is established by what we can reproduce, which is the account of identity Level B needs.

05 / The geometric bit

Shannon’s bit is a reading of it

Recording which side of a diameter holds the mark gives one bit, and two perpendicular diameters give four quadrants, the four corners of the square, whose labels we can keep while setting the exact angle aside.

Once those alternatives and their probabilities are given, Shannon’s measure applies to the choice among them. The bit is the circle read at its quadrants, with the turn and the reference set aside, and the circle keeps both.

06 / Shannon's bit is the first row

Shannon's bit is the first row

  • Centre the bit: −1 and +1, joined by the half turn eiπ = −1.
  • Squaring the circle sends z and −z to one point: the fibre S⁰, a bit's two alternatives.
  • The Hopf table: each whole space becomes the next fibre. The circle is the second row's fibre.
  • Structural fractality; the sedenions end it.

Gödel, with the checker put back

  • The proof needs Gödel at every step: to build the coding, to read the sentence as self-referring, to see that it is true.

  • Turing (1939) and Feferman (1962): the open tower of checks reaches every true sentence of arithmetic.
